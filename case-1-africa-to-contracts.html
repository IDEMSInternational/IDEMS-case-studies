From STACK in Africa to commercial contracts

A small pilot in Kenya began in 2019 because one lecturer had a thousand students and no way to give them feedback. Seven years later it has grown into a pan-African community and a paid assessment service for universities in the US and Italy. IDEMS has been part of it throughout: first doing the work, then building the people and partnerships to carry it, and now offering that expertise as a paid service. The students, lecturers, interns and leaders behind it appear here as fictional personas.

IDEMS' role at a glance

IDEMS International is a UK-registered community interest company working on education, data and technology. Its role has changed as local capacity has grown, moving from doing the work to backing others who do it.

  • 2019 Initiated and delivered the Maseno pilot: IDEMS staff wrote the questions and set up the quizzes.
  • 2022 Main funder and host of the Maseno workshop, hosting courses for seven universities that had no Moodle.
  • 2022–23 Co-created the internship with INNODEMS, its Kenyan counterpart, and trained the first interns.
  • 2023 Co-designed Ethiopia's STACK remedial course with Bahir Dar University.
  • 2023–26 Co-organised the African STACK conferences and led their hands-on authoring workshops.
  • 2025 Formal partner through an MoU with Bahir Dar and Wollo universities, committed to supporting universities in South Sudan and Somalia.
  • 2025– Service provider: IDEMS staff deliver the paid contracts in the US and Italy.

A problem of scale

In Kenya, first-year service courses such as Calculus or Linear Algebra regularly enrol 500 to 1,200 students. In 2019 the School of Mathematics at Maseno University had 16 staff, each teaching four or more courses without assistants. A lecturer in that position can set one or two continuous assessment tests a semester, because that is all they can mark. Students cram before those tests and get almost no feedback in between.

That is the life of Samuel Okello, the Overwhelmed Lecturer. Samuel knows his students need weekly practice and feedback. He is one person with 900 scripts.

“I have nine hundred students in Calculus and I mark every script myself. I know they need practice and feedback every week, but I am one man. There has to be a better way.”

STACK, the open-source assessment system for mathematics developed at the University of Edinburgh, offered one. Its questions are randomised, so each student gets a different version. It checks answers algebraically rather than by string matching, and it can give tailored feedback on specific mistakes. In 2019 IDEMS proposed a pilot to Maseno and delivered it, on Edinburgh's servers, in Calculus I and Introduction to Linear Algebra. IDEMS staff wrote the questions and built the quizzes, and Maseno lecturers ran the courses. Each week students first took a mastery quiz, with unlimited attempts and an 80% threshold, which then unlocked a single-attempt test quiz. Together these counted for 30% of the course grade.

The pilot worked, and it also showed what would shape everything that followed. Most students did their quizzes on their own smartphones using mobile data they had paid for, because campus Wi-Fi was unreliable. Questions had to be written fast, which left little time for detailed feedback, and some quizzes came out late. In a survey of 470 Linear Algebra students, they valued the multiple attempts and the weekly rhythm, and asked for better feedback and more accessible questions.

What students actually did

A 2022 study of the 2021 Complex Analysis course at Maseno (Juma, Ayere, Oyengo and Osang) went further. It combined quiz logs, exam results, interviews and focus groups, and sorted students into groups by how they engaged with the quizzes and how they then did in the exam. Several of the personas are built on those groups.

The most uncomfortable finding was about students with high quiz scores who failed the exam. Seventeen of twenty interviewees and thirty-one of thirty-two focus-group participants said students paid or "booked" stronger classmates to do their quizzes. They described it as ordinary "help". Brian Wafula is a composite of that experience. He has an evening job, the quizzes keep coming, and he only realises in the exam what he has given up. On the other side is Grace Nangolo, the High Achiever, who finishes the quiz on the first day and is then asked to do other people's.

Other groups are more hopeful. Some students had weak weekly scores but passed by going back over the mastery quizzes before the exam. Dawit Bekele, the Quiet Reviser, uses the practice quizzes as a self-study course with feedback. Others made many attempts and still struggled in the exam, partly because the feedback skipped steps. Neema Mollel keeps trying new variants, hoping one will show her the step she is missing. Some students, like Ivan Ssekandi, did "just the minimum to move forward". Around one in five struggled with access: data ran out, the Wi-Fi was down, or the outdoor Wi-Fi area had no shelter from the rain. Mercy Akinyi represents them.

“My bundle finished, so I ran to the Wi-Fi area, and the Wi-Fi was also down. Then it started raining and there is no shelter there. That week I just left the quiz.”

Even so, 19 of 20 interviewees wanted STACK in all their maths courses. The study turned a vague sense that "STACK helps" into specific design questions. How do we make cheating less tempting? How do we write feedback that shows every step? How do we stop access problems from becoming grade problems? Those questions still drive the work.

Building people, not just questions

A pilot cannot grow if every question has to be written by a few people abroad. In July 2022 a five-day workshop at Maseno brought together about 49 lecturers and interns from 14 institutions in Kenya, Tanzania and Ethiopia, on a budget of under US$10,000. The plan was to present existing courses as templates. By day two, the lecturers had asked to change the agenda. They wanted to learn to write STACK questions themselves. They found it "time-consuming, requires a little bit of ICT experience and hard work", and very exciting. IDEMS was the workshop's main funder and covered its own staff's travel, and a University of Geneva research fund raised participation from about 20 to about 50. Seven of the universities had no Moodle at all, so IDEMS agreed to host their courses on its own servers, as it had done for Dar es Salaam and Maseno since 2019.

Tesfaye Alemu, the Enthusiastic New Lecturer, comes out of moments like this: thrilled by his first working randomised question, then back home with no mentor and no Wi-Fi. Joseph Kamau, the ICT administrator whose phone rings when a thousand students open a quiz at 11.55 pm, stands for the infrastructure behind it.

The answer to "no one to ask" was the INNODEMS internship, which IDEMS set up with INNODEMS, its Kenyan counterpart organisation. From July 2022, four recent graduates were trained by experienced IDEMS staff to write and review STACK questions, manage Moodle and support lecturers. They learned LaTeX, HTML and JavaScript on the job, with remote mentors whose working culture was very different from their own. Each question went through four layers of review. In one year they helped put eight courses at Masinde Muliro University of Science and Technology (MMUST) and Maseno onto STACK, built six open question banks of about 150 questions each, and supported a remedial programme in Ethiopia. Lecturers saw the difference. At Maseno, weekly homework with instant feedback meant a 1,000-student Linear Algebra class could run without teaching assistants, and exam performance improved. At MMUST, results in Complex Analysis rose after STACK was introduced.

By 2025 this had become a lasting model, with leadership increasingly in Kenya. IDEMS stayed involved through joint leadership, mentoring and technical development, while day-to-day support moved to the Kenyan team. INNODEMS grew from four interns to fourteen staff and supported at least seven institutions in five countries, 20+ courses and over 10,000 students a year. It rests on three pillars: shared question banks (about 200 questions in each of seven areas), technical support (Moodle, STACK, migration, maintenance), and training (workshops for lecturers and TVET tutors, peer learning and online mentoring). Daniel Mensah, the Local Technical Intern, stands for that career path, and also for its insecurity.

“Six months ago I didn’t know what Maxima was. Now lecturers call me to fix their quizzes and I’ve written questions that thousands of students use. I just hope there’s a job at the end of it.”

Champions also matter. Dr. Rehema Salim, the STACK Champion, is a composite of the lecturers at Dar es Salaam, Maseno and MMUST who stayed with STACK for years, moved whole courses onto it and now train colleagues. Their biggest worry is no longer the technology. It is burnout, and what happens to the work when they move on.

Crisis as a catalyst: Ethiopia, 2023

Ethiopia showed how fast STACK can be useful when a whole system is under pressure. In 2023 only about 3% of school leavers passed the national secondary exit exam. Universities were told to run remedial programmes for those who failed, and they were also preparing for the first national exit exams for graduating students. A workshop at Bahir Dar University in March 2023, run by IDEMS and Bahir Dar staff with 49 participants from 12 Ethiopian universities, agreed three priorities. The first was a STACK remedial course, co-designed by IDEMS and Bahir Dar University, covering 13 topics released week by week, for up to 100,000 students. The second was exit-exam preparation that reused existing Kenyan and Tanzanian questions to cover 50–75% of the exam blueprints straight away. The third was a redesign of the compulsory first-year course for up to 1,500 students in its first pilot. The remedial programme covered its whole curriculum in six weeks.

Dr. Hayat Seid, the Head of Department, is the person who has to make this work. Every first-year student takes her course. Policy changes arrive with weeks' notice, and she has seen many projects disappear when their funding ends. What convinces her is evidence, shared materials and formal agreements. Two years later a Memorandum of Understanding between Bahir Dar University, Wollo University and IDEMS made the collaboration formal.

“Every first-year student in this university takes Basic Mathematics. STACK looks promising, but I have seen many projects come and go. I need it to work for 6,000 students, and after the funding ends.”

A community, not a project

From 2023 the work had a yearly gathering place. The conferences were organised by African host universities together with IDEMS and INNODEMS, and IDEMS staff led the hands-on authoring workshops at each one. Each conference made the community a bit more formal and widened who it included.

  • 2023 First African STACK Conference at MMUST: 66 participants from 16 institutions, on about US$9,700. It set up the African STACK Community Leadership Team, a partnership with the Kenya Mathematical Society and a commitment to annual conferences in rotating venues.
  • 2024 Second conference at the Technical University of Kenya: 70+ participants, including TVET institutions for the first time. Topics included the five-quiz model, proof questions and maths anxiety. Participants agreed to develop certification for STACK users.
  • 2025 Third conference at Bahir Dar: 78+ courses now integrated, 11 African countries present, an MoU signed with IDEMS, IDEMS commitments to support universities in Juba and Mogadishu, and interest from Somalia, South Sudan, Namibia, Ghana, Cameroon and Botswana.
  • 2026 International STACK meeting in Nairobi: about 75 participants from 17 countries. Topics included generative AI for writing questions, entering maths on phones, uneven adoption, academic integrity and competency-based curricula. A Ghanaian partner offered to host in 2027.

Each new group needed its own persona. Esther Wanjiku, the TVET Tutor, and Peter Kiprono, a trainee electrician, show that technical colleges have different maths needs, different devices and different motivation. Clarisse Mbarga is an older mechanic returning to study, and French-speaking. Dr. Ibrahim El-Tayeb represents leaders bringing STACK into conflict-affected systems. At the top of universities, Prof. Njeri Wambui wants to know whether STACK will still be running in five years.

The 2026 meeting also brought up a harder truth. At MMUST, now running STACK in 20+ courses, some lecturers teaching parallel groups of the same course were using it and some were not, so students in the same course had very different experiences. That is the world of Dr. Yusuf Mohamed, the Reluctant Lecturer. He is not against technology, but he is unconvinced while quiz marks and exam marks still disagree. The meeting's main conclusion was that providing the technology is not enough to scale STACK. It also takes "broad staff participation, consistent implementation practices and sustained institutional support".

“I have taught this course on the blackboard for 25 years and my students pass. My colleague’s group uses STACK; mine does not. If it is so good, why do their exam results not match their quiz marks?”

Funding runs through every stage of this story. Conferences have been paid for by many small contributions from the European Mathematical Society, universities in Europe and the US, mathematics charities and participants themselves. Ruth Achieng, the Programme Officer, and Charles Mwakasege, the Philanthropist, are the funders whose questions a long-term model must be able to answer. Will this last? Who owns it locally? What did the money actually do?

Crossing over: the commercial contracts

The step from community work to paid work came through people, not marketing. IDEMS' track record in Africa was what both clients had seen. A mathematician at the University of Trieste had seen STACK through IDEMS' Kenyan partners and wanted it in Italy. IDEMS was first subcontracted to write Trieste's first questions, starting with statistics for biology students. Trieste then found it cheaper to employ a local author, trained through that work. Lecturers there began writing questions on their own as well as with IDEMS. The relationship has now grown into a wider contract: IDEMS reviews and improves the questions Trieste wrote, especially in Linear Algebra, and turns more of the department's paper exercises into STACK questions.

Three personas reflect this. Giulia Moretti is a biology student who only sees why statistics matters when the questions use data from experiments like hers. Luca Ferrara is in a Linear Algebra class with a very wide range of ability. Prof. Paolo Benedetti built a large question bank in Italian and now wants it checked, made consistent and extended.

“We started by asking IDEMS for statistics questions for our biologists. Then I learned to write my own. Now we have hundreds, and some are not as good as they should be.”

The second contract is with a highly selective US research university. It began in 2025 with a large required applied-mathematics course on ordinary and partial differential equations: 130 to 190 students from many departments, undergraduates and PhD students together, in ten weeks. Content that used to take three terms had been squeezed into two. There was almost no existing STACK material. Instructors wrote 43 new questions in LaTeX, and IDEMS, with colleagues at Edinburgh, converted them to STACK. Another 11 were adapted from ETH Zurich. IDEMS also ran the STACK server and analysed student responses after the course. Every question had two rounds of review. The university used a different learning management system from Moodle, so connecting the two took months of work with its IT teams.

Students at a highly selective university behave differently from a class of a thousand in Kenya, but not in every way. At first they were frustrated by the default "deferred feedback" mode, where they only learn whether they were right after submitting the whole quiz, and they did not trust automatic marking. Two changes turned this around. Switching to adaptive mode, with immediate feedback and retries, was rated "very useful" by 86% of students. A regrade request process gave them a fair way to appeal. Anonymous surveys after every quiz, and quick action on them, built trust over the term. Students valued the graded weekly quizzes most. Questions written later in the term were better received than early ones. Since then the contract has been extended to Calculus 1 and Linear Algebra.

Marcus Alvarez, the High-Flying Undergraduate, and Mei Lin Chen, the PhD Student from Another Field, are the students. Dr. Daniel Brooks is the instructor redesigning a course against the clock. Priya Raman is the head teaching assistant who receives every regrade request.

“I don’t mind hard questions. What I mind is submitting and not knowing if I’m right until next week, or losing points because a computer didn’t like how I wrote my answer.”

Both contracts are delivered by IDEMS staff, not by the African team. The persona of Halima Bello, the IDEMS Question Author, captures the tension: client deadlines in the morning, open question banks for African universities in the evening.

What is the same, and what is different

It would be easy to assume that a 1,000-student course in Kenya and a 150-student course in the US need different things. The experience so far suggests otherwise. STACK plays a very different role in each. In Kenya it makes regular feedback possible where there is no one else to give it. At the US university there are teaching assistants and tutors, so people are not the bottleneck. Yet in both places students value the same thing: low-stakes mastery practice, with multiple attempts, different variants and feedback on each attempt. The question types that help most are the same too. Formative questions that build mastery are the best place to start almost everywhere.

The real difference is what each setting can afford. When questions were written as unpaid impact work for low-resource universities, quality had to be traded for quantity: many questions, little time for feedback. Paid work allows the three-cycle process STACK's developers recommend, where a question is written, used, analysed from real student responses and then improved. The commercial work therefore raises the standard IDEMS can offer everyone. Because most partners agree to open licences, questions written for one institution can be adapted for another.

The contracts have also changed IDEMS' own model. Most STACK expertise sits inside individual universities: the STACK professionals network has 20–30 active members, all based at an institution except IDEMS. IDEMS is becoming something different, a cross-institution team of assessment specialists who work alongside in-house authors rather than replacing them. The team mixes former secondary teachers and mathematicians whose research is in other areas. They work with subject experts who write the specifications, and they can author good questions in topics outside their own expertise. The contracts are also starting to cover the cost of the people doing the work. That makes the work in Africa more sustainable, and it is the business model that Ruth and Charles would ask about.

What we learned

  • Feedback is better than none, even when it isn't perfect. One MMUST student retook a quiz 18 times and eventually got it. Better feedback should get them there sooner.
  • Access problems become grade problems unless the design allows for them. Phones, data bundles and sheltered Wi-Fi are part of assessment design.
  • Cheating follows high stakes. Low-stakes mastery, with exam-like checks elsewhere, works better than trying to police quizzes.
  • Local capacity is what makes the work last. Interns became staff, champions became trainers, and peer learning spread faster than outside experts could.
  • Settings matter as much as content. Adaptive mode and a regrade route changed how the US class felt about STACK.
  • Scaling is about people. Uneven adoption between parallel groups is the next barrier, not the technology.
  • Commercial and impact work strengthen each other. Paid work funds quality, and open licences let that quality reach low-resource partners.

What comes next

The next stage builds on both halves of the story. INNODEMS plans regional support hubs, more work with TVET institutions and polytechnics, and expansion into Francophone, Southern and conflict-affected Africa. The 2027 meeting is planned for Ghana. Commercially, IDEMS is extending its existing contracts to new courses and exploring a role as a shared assessment service that universities can draw on without each building one of their own. Two further threads grow out of this case study. One is a move from quizzes inside learning management systems to interactive textbooks that reach students who never log in to Moodle (case study 2). The other is AI tools that make question writing fast and approachable enough for many more authors (case study 3).
