Product development: AI agents for writing STACK questions

Every chapter of the STACK journey has run into the same limit: too few people who can write good questions. This case study follows how IDEMS went from a personal chatbot to a multi-agent system that plans, writes and checks questions with a human author. It works across whole question banks and delivers to Moodle, PreTeXt books and beyond.

IDEMS' role

IDEMS designs, builds and runs the system described here, and uses it every day on its own work: commercial contracts, open question banks and textbook assessment. Building on the STACK community's work, IDEMS is also developing its approach to responsible AI: tools that make people more capable, are open about every step, and can run on any model.

The bottleneck

Writing a STACK question is not one hard skill but many fairly simple ones done together. An author needs some HTML, some Maxima (STACK's computer algebra language), an understanding of how STACK checks answers and branches to different feedback, sensible randomisation, and, most of all, the teaching judgement to write good feedback. Few people have all of these. Even experts find it slow.

The bottleneck shows up throughout this story. Lecturers at the 2022 Maseno workshop found authoring "time-consuming". The INNODEMS interns needed months of steep learning (case study 1). In 2025, demand for support was already greater than INNODEMS could meet. Commercial contracts come with deadlines. And as interactive textbooks multiply, every book needs questions (case study 2). Meanwhile most teachers are used to setting "page 42, exercises 1 to 10". About 2,000 institutions use STACK worldwide. The gap between that and every institution that could benefit is mainly a gap in authors.

Chiara Fontana is the persona for this gap: a motivated secondary teacher with a full-time job and three children, who loves the idea and cannot see how she would ever start.

“I love the idea: practice with feedback for every student. But I have a full-time job and three children. If I have to learn HTML, Maxima and feedback trees first, I’ll never begin.”

Stage 1: a chatbot beside the editor

The work began before its developer, Michele Pancera, joined IDEMS. A former secondary maths teacher who learned STACK while working for the University of Trieste, he built a personal assistant on a commercial chatbot platform. He trained it on STACK documentation and example questions. It produced question variables, question text and general feedback (Maxima code and HTML that work together), which he copied into Moodle by hand. It was useful in two ways. It acted as interactive documentation for someone still learning, and it made him perhaps five to ten times faster. Questions also came out tidier, with comments, and could easily be multilingual.

When IDEMS adopted the assistant, its STACK authors used it every day on contract work. Santiago Borio said on the IDEMS podcast that he had not written a single question without it since. He described it as improving both speed and quality, but only because he knew STACK well enough to correct it. It sometimes made up syntax, for example for the function that checks multiple-choice answers, until he told it to check the documentation. It went round in circles on an old Parsons proof question until he asked whether it was using outdated code. It turned out it was. It was also very good at the dull but valuable jobs: adding clear comments to hard-to-read variables in older questions, and checking a revised question against a lecturer's list of requested changes, where it found one he had missed.

Halima Bello, the IDEMS Question Author, and Claire Dubois represent this stage: experts for whom AI enhances their skill rather than replacing it.

Stage 2: what changed

Two years later, two things had changed. First, the models themselves improved. Earlier they could not reliably edit STACK's XML question files, where the trees that branch to different feedback are almost unreadable to a person. That is why the first assistant produced snippets for copying. Now editing XML is routine, and models can keep a whole set of questions consistent. Second, models became agents. They can work directly in a repository, call tools, check their own output and fix their mistakes.

Ben Davies and Adam Clearwater at the University of Southampton showed what this made possible. In their published case study, supervised agents working in a coding assistant built a school textbook's worth of STACK questions for the SMP A-level books. That raised a design question that IDEMS is now asking across all its AI work: when are more agents worth the cost? As Michele Pancera put it on the IDEMS podcast: "if you have a kitchen filled with 50 people, cooking a dish of pasta is going to be more difficult than with one." Splitting work between agents only helps if the parts can work without the whole context. A separate agent just for the feedback trees looked attractive, but it would need the whole question as context anyway. And a loop between one agent and the STACK validator is not a multi-agent system at all.

Stage 3: two systems, one repository

What IDEMS has built is really two systems that share the same question banks, documentation and tools.

The interface: several agents working with a human author

Each question bank is a git repository. A question is a folder containing the standard Moodle XML file, a one-page description with fixed fields (goal, description, structure, randomisation, answer tests, feedback, implementation notes, additional languages) and a log that records every change. Work is organised as issues: a parent issue for a review, say, and one sub-issue per question. Each piece of work happens on its own branch and closes its issue when it is merged. This is the same way IDEMS' software teams have always worked, which keeps every decision visible to the human team.

  • Concierge Reads every configured bank: questions, descriptions, logs, open issues. Answers questions such as "how many multiple-choice questions does this bank have?" and drafts and files the issues for the work.
  • Planner Works with the author on a detailed plan in the same fixed fields. It runs at higher effort so the other agents can run at lower effort.
  • Retriever A small agent that chooses which documentation the Author sees.
  • Author Edits the question field by field, never touching the raw XML. It checks the question with the STACK API, fixes errors using STACK's own messages, and reports back.
  • Human Watches a live preview, answers the question, edits it by hand, confirms or rejects test results and decides whether to merge.

The design choices are about cost and control. Converting XML into fields that agents edit like a person editing the Moodle form keeps prompts short, while the files stay standard Moodle XML. Instead of the full STACK documentation, the agents use concise cheat-sheets: 128 relevant pages have been selected and 62 rewritten so far, each listing first the mistakes that fail silently. Choosing which sheets to load, through a fixed table, the Planner's suggestions and the Retriever, cut the Author's system prompt from 25,900 to 11,400 tokens. Each agent can use a different model, set in the interface. Banks can stay in sync with Moodle through the gitsync plugin, so a revised question arrives in Moodle as a new version of the existing one rather than a duplicate.

The coding assistant: the same repository, for experts

The second way in is to open a general coding assistant in the same repository. There is no preview or editor, but it has every cheat-sheet, tool, rule and bank. It suits work outside the authoring cycle, such as "create a question tracker for this bank, then a report on the work done since last month". Each system improves the other. While reviewing questions with the coding assistant, the team found that Moodle's importer silently filled in missing values, and in one STACK version this set every incorrect branch's score to 1. The fix went into the code the agents use to write questions. Randomised Venn diagrams worked out in one coding-assistant session became a cheat-sheet and two examples that the Retriever can now give the Author. Rules that colleagues state during reviews are collected in one file that both systems read.

Dr. Owen Price, the Expert Who Drives the Agents, works mostly through the coding assistant. Claire Dubois works in the interface. Dr. Fiona MacLeod, a STACK professional employed by a university, sees both an opportunity and a threat to her job.

“I’m a former secondary teacher writing PDE questions I couldn’t solve myself. The lecturer writes the spec, the Planner and I turn it into a plan, the Author builds it, and I check every branch before it merges.”

Results so far

Together the two systems have been used to create or rework more than 120 questions in six banks. They supported IDEMS' Linear Algebra work for the University of Trieste (case study 1) and the conversion of a bank of 80 multiple-choice review questions. They also matched an open statistics bank to a PreTeXt textbook, selecting about 40 questions, half of them close matches (case study 2).

An audit of 32 questions from the Trieste Linear Algebra batch shows the kind of quality gain involved:

  • All 32 pass validation with the STACK API, import safely and pass their question tests.
  • There were 51 branches, across 15 questions, where students lost marks with no explanation. Each now gives a message.
  • Four branches that carry an earlier error through to later parts were added, each with a test to catch future breakage.
  • Five questions that previously could not be tested now can be.
  • Proof questions moved from all-or-nothing marking to partial credit.

For Luca Ferrara in Trieste, and for Abdikarim Yusuf in a course whose questions were reviewed the same way, the difference is simple: a wrong answer now comes with an explanation.

“Last term the quiz just said ‘incorrect’. This term, when I make the same mistake, it tells me which step went wrong. I don’t know who changed it. I only know it helps.”

The subject expert's role is still central. Prof. Paolo Benedetti wants his own bank checked and extended. Dr. Samuel Adeyemi writes the specification and alone decides what counts as "close enough". The plan, with its fixed fields, is where his judgement goes in. Emma Hartley uses the system to match questions across textbooks, then makes the final decision herself.

Two kinds of AI: for experts, and for building expertise

Building both systems side by side brought out a distinction that now shapes IDEMS' thinking about AI. A general coding assistant driven by an expert is very powerful. The agents have a lot of freedom, the expert catches them when they go off track, and one skilled person can achieve what used to take a team. That is how Owen works. But taken to its logical end, that model does not need teachers at all.

The interface takes the other approach. Its agents have narrow, well-defined roles, the workflow is fixed, every step is visible as an issue or a branch, and the human approves at set points. It is designed so that people who are not yet experts can do good work, and learn while they do it. Dr. Lucy Njoroge, a Kenyan lecturer who never had time to learn STACK properly, can describe what she wants her students to practise, check the plan and watch the question appear in the preview. IDEMS sees this kind of AI, which serves many people in their own settings, as getting much less attention than it deserves, and as central to the jobs of the future.

“I never had time to learn STACK properly. With the planner I describe what I want my students to practise, check the plan, and watch it appear in the preview. I’m learning STACK by doing it.”

The hope for Chiara is concrete. Within a few months she might have assessment for the textbook she already uses, with her input at the centre. Within a year she might be contributing to open question banks as part of a network of teachers, where the AI suggests "someone has improved this question, do you want to look?" The quality would rise because of the teachers, with the AI as, in the words of IDEMS director David Stern, "the oil that makes the machine work".

Responsible by design

Right now the agents need models at roughly the level of Claude Sonnet 5, and few models are at that level. That raises questions of cost, dependence on a few providers, and energy and water use. The system is built to be model-agnostic so these can be managed. Each agent can run on a different model or provider. The Planner does the heavy thinking so the Author can run cheaply. Small edits can be made by hand with no model at all. The approach mirrors one used by partners in other fields: use the strongest models now to build systems that can later run on smaller, cheaper and eventually open models, possibly small enough for a laptop or a phone. IDEMS is also moving towards open-source git hosting with Forgejo.

These questions matter most to the people at the edges. Yaw Darko, technical lead at a new West African hub, wants to run the system for his universities but cannot pay a dollar per question in foreign currency indefinitely. Jonas Weber, a funder, wants AI that makes teachers more capable and a plan for cost and energy that outlasts the next generation of models.

“We want to run the system for our universities, but every question costs a dollar of API calls and the best models are foreign and expensive. Show me a path to cheaper models and our own git server.”

Beyond questions: adapting whole textbooks

The same approach of specialist agents with people in charge now also supports IDEMS' work on textbook variants (case study 2). Agents help translate content, switch a book from one programming language to another, adapt examples to a new context, and apply a correction across every linked version of a book. As with questions, they do the routine work at scale. Authors decide what they want, subject experts review the results, and people remain responsible for what is published.

Challenges

  • Moodle with gitsync. To use the system fully, banks need to stay in sync with a Moodle site that has the gitsync plugin. Without it, questions are imported by hand and arrive as new questions rather than new versions.
  • Few models at the required level. Smaller models can be selected, but are not yet expected to handle hard questions.
  • Question tests are hard for AI to write. Tests may target answers that the input rejects before marking, or be meant to fail but pass for some random variants. So a failing test never blocks a question on its own: a person confirms or rejects the result.
  • People must stay in charge. The 2026 Nairobi meeting stressed this for every AI-assisted authoring approach discussed. The workflow builds it in: plans are approved, previews are checked and merges are decided by people.

Beyond authoring: proof

AI also changes what can be assessed. Proof has long been STACK's weak point. Parsons problems, where students drag steps of a proof into order, came partly from accepting that writing proofs could not be marked automatically, and partly from research suggesting that understanding proofs is often the better learning goal. IDEMS' proof questions usually add follow-up questions about understanding for this reason. With Chris Sangwin's recent work on free-text input in STACK, and AI's growing ability to read mathematical writing, automatic assessment of written proofs may be within reach in the next year or so. The open question then becomes a teaching question rather than a technical one: what balance of reading and writing proofs actually helps students learn?

What we learned

  • Expertise still matters. The chatbot sped experts up and improved their work, but it made up syntax that only an expert could catch.
  • Structure is how non-experts get help. Narrow agent roles, fixed plans, previews and human approval let less experienced authors do good work and learn from it.
  • Reuse the team's existing workflow. Issues, branches and logs make AI work transparent to a human team.
  • Choosing context saves money. Fields instead of XML, cheat-sheets instead of full documentation and a Retriever cut costs sharply.
  • Improvements are shared. Anything learned in one system (a cheat-sheet, a rule, a bug fix) benefits the other.
  • Be model-agnostic from the start. It is what will allow cheaper, smaller and open models later.
  • The biggest quality gains are often unglamorous. Giving feedback where students used to lose marks in silence helps them immediately.

What comes next

The next steps are:

  • Letting users upload their own files as context for the agents.
  • Supporting question banks hosted on Forgejo as well as GitHub.
  • Offering the system to others beyond IDEMS.

The longer-term aim connects all three case studies. The question banks built in Africa and improved through contracts, and the questions placed in textbooks, could be maintained and extended by a wide network of lecturers, interns and teachers, with AI handling the routine work so they can focus on teaching. If that works, the people at the start of this story, Samuel with his 900 scripts and Tesfaye with no mentor, would no longer need to become STACK experts. The tools would do that part for them.
